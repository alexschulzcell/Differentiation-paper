Dear Editors,

Please consider our manuscript "What skeletal differentiation models actually
measure: a lineage-independent matrix programme, and why the disease genes lie
orthogonal to it" for consideration as a Research Article in *iScience*.

Mesenchymal differentiation models are the standard readout for how skeletal
dysplasia genes disturb bone and cartilage formation, yet the hope that these
many lesions converge on one transcriptional downstream signal has rarely been
tested against an explicit noise expectation or a stated detection limit. We
built the test around two requirements that are usually absent: a positive
control internal to each dataset, showing whether the culture actually reached
the lineage it models, and a measured detection limit for every negative
result. A level whose calibration fails is reported as not measurable, never
as no effect.

Applying it, only 2 of 18 published perturbation datasets pass their own
calibration, while a fixed 173-gene matrix programme runs above its own
detection limit in all 18, runs also on the adipogenic lineage in chromatin,
and tracks the human fetal growth plate to the prehypertrophic zone. The
disease genes lie orthogonal to this programme: they are defined by distal
secretory localisation and gene dosage, and they are ordinary on the
differentiation axis, with none of the 40 computable mechanism-class tests
exceeding Bonferroni correction. The two layers meet at exactly one place, the
prehypertrophic transition, which is what we propose measuring instead of a
transcriptional downstream marker.

*iScience* is the right home for this work: it joins human genetics,
chromatin biology, single-cell atlases and reanalysis methodology in one
argument, and its negatives carry measured detection limits rather than
rhetoric, which suits the journal's required Limitations of the Study
section. Three earlier framings of this question
failed against their own controls; they are reported in full, together with
the preregistrations that document them.

All primary data are public and listed with accession numbers in Supplementary
Table 1, including our own urine-derived stem cell series under E-MTAB-16566;
the analysis code is publicly available in a GitHub repository, and a self-test script reproduces
every number quoted in the manuscript. The study generated no new human
material and no new human data, so no separate institutional review board
approval was required. It has not been published elsewhere and is not under
consideration elsewhere, and we declare no competing interests.

We would be glad to suggest reviewers with expertise in skeletal dysplasia
genetics, mesenchymal differentiation models or reanalysis methodology, and we
have no reviewer exclusions to request.
